## Work Session: Reading the Hatch Marks

The little lines on a ruler are **hatch marks**. The two sides of your ruler count in completely different ways, and mixing them up is where measurements go wrong.

### U.S. Customary — count sixteenths

One inch is split into 16 equal parts. Count the marks, then reduce the fraction if you can — 4/16 is a real answer, but 1/4 is how an engineer writes it.

| Marks you counted | Same thing, reduced |
| --- | --- |
| 1/16 | 1/16 |
| 2/16 | 1/8 |
| 4/16 | 1/4 |
| 8/16 | 1/2 |
| 12/16 | 3/4 |
| 16/16 | 1 inch |

### Metric — count tenths

One centimeter is split into 10 equal parts, written as decimals: 0.1 cm, 0.2 cm, all the way to 1 cm. No fractions on this side of the ruler.

{{< callout type="important" >}}
Inches use **fractions**. Centimeters use **decimals**. Do not mix them — "1.2 inches" is not how this gets written, and it is not how the skimmer sketch you build from tomorrow is labeled.
{{< /callout >}}

### Where the measurement starts

- Line the edge of the object up with the **starting hatch mark**.
- Some rulers print a 0. Some do not. Find the first mark and use it.
- Look straight down at the ruler. Reading from an angle changes your number.

{{< callout type="tip" >}}
Starting at the plastic edge of the ruler instead of the first hatch mark is the single most common measuring mistake. Check where your zero actually is before you measure anything.
{{< /callout >}}

### Steps 6–9: Practice in inches

1. Draw a triangle in your notebook with two sides that each measure **1-3/16 inches**. The angle between them is your choice.
2. Measure the third side.
3. Label all three sides — on the **outside** of the triangle so they do not crowd the drawing.
4. Trade with a classmate. Re-measure their three sides yourself; do not just look at their numbers.

## Work Session: Why Didn't Everybody Get the Same Third Side?

Compare your third side with the people around you. The numbers will not match — and there are two different reasons that can happen. Engineers have to know which one they are looking at.

Design Variation
: You each picked your own angle between the two given sides. A different angle gives a different third side. Nobody did anything wrong — the design was different.

Measurement Error
: Two people measure the **same** side and get different numbers. That one is a mistake — somebody read the ruler wrong, started at the wrong place, or rounded when they should not have.

### Step 14: Reading the skimmer dimensions

We look at the **Skimmer Dimensions** sketches together. These are the exact sketches you build from tomorrow, so be able to answer:

- What unit of measure is used? How can you tell just by looking?
- How long is the main body?
- How wide is it unfolded? How wide after it is folded along the dotted lines?
- On the air scoop, what do the 1/8 dimensions represent? What about the 3/8 dimensions?

{{< callout type="important" >}}
The **dotted lines** on the skimmer sketch are **fold lines, not cut lines.** Cutting along a dotted line ruins the part. You will hear this again tomorrow.

## Closing

Today: two systems of measurement, how to read hatch marks on both sides of a ruler, and how to read a sketch that has dimensions on it.

Tomorrow is build day. You draw the four skimmer parts on a file folder from those exact dimensions, get your drawing checked before you cut anything, then cut, fold, and assemble.

Fractions of an inch matter here. A part that is off by 1/8 of an inch, or a fold in the wrong place, is a skimmer that nose-dives instead of flying.
